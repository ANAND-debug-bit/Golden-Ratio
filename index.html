Worth saying plainly: a low score here is not a verdict on your face. The golden ratio is one 15th-century rule of thumb borrowed from architecture — plenty of widely admired faces miss it by a wide margin. Read this as a geometry lesson with your face as the diagram, not as a grade.

How the measurement actually works

Six ratios, one number.

The page finds 68 points on your face , the same scheme ophthalmologists and animators both use , then takes six measurements between them that classical proportion theory cares about: things like face length against face width, or the width of your mouth against the width of your nose. Each one is compared to Φ (Golden Number) (1.618) or to a known ideal ratio, and the six results are averaged into the score you see above.

1.618

Face length ÷ face width

Hairline to chin, divided by the distance between your cheekbones. The classical "ideal" oval comes from this single ratio.

1.618

Pupil span ÷ nose width

Distance between the centres of your eyes, divided by the width of your nose at its base.

1.618

Mouth width ÷ nose width

A wider mouth relative to the nose is one of the oldest "balanced face" rules in figure drawing.

1.618

Face length ÷ eye-to-chin

From the line between your eyes down to your chin, compared against your whole face length.

1.0

Eye width ÷ inter-eye gap

The old studio rule that one eye's width should roughly equal the gap between your eyes.

1.618

Lip-to-chin ÷ nose-to-lip

The lower third of the face, split at the mouth, checked against the same golden division.
